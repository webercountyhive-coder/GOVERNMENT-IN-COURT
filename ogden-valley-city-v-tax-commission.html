CASE FILE · GOVERNMENT IN COURT · WEBER COUNTY · GC 002

One day late: Ogden Valley City v. the Utah State Tax Commission

Utah's newest city got its certificate of incorporation on Jan. 2, 2026. The Tax Commission says a city must exist by Jan. 1 to levy property tax that year. A judge agreed, and the city has appealed to the Utah Supreme Court.

Courts: Second and Third District; Utah Supreme CourtCase No. 260903949Filed June 4, 2026Status: appeal filed; later docket not verified

Posted Nov. 6, 2026

WHERE · WHO
Ogden Valley City (Weber County) · Utah State Tax Commission · Utah Supreme Court
  • Who is suing: Ogden Valley City, represented pro bono by Gordon Law Group (city attorney Corbin Gordon).
  • Who is being sued: The Utah State Tax Commission.
  • What it is about: Whether a city certified on Jan. 2 can levy its own property tax for 2026.
  • The judges: Catherine Conklin (Second District) signed the TRO; Todd Shaughnessy (Third District, a tax judge) ruled on the merits.
  • Money at stake: About $2.48 million a year under the city's proposed 512.6% increase.

In plain words (explain it like I'm 10)

A new city has to be "born" by New Year's Day to collect property tax that year. Ogden Valley's birth certificate came on Jan. 2, one day late. The city's paperwork was turned in Dec. 17, and the city says the delay wasn't its fault. A judge agreed it wasn't the city's fault, but still said the rule is the rule. So for now the city runs on a small budget without its own property tax. It asked the Utah Supreme Court to change that.

The short version

  • The rule: a Tax Commission rule (R884-24P-24) says a new entity must exist by Jan. 1 to levy tax that year. The city's certificate was issued Jan. 2, 2026 (KUER; Ogden Valley News).
  • The paperwork: submitted Dec. 17, 2025; a missing signature or notary fixed Dec. 23; certificate issued Jan. 2 (Ogden Valley News).
  • First ruling: June 22, Judge Catherine Conklin signed a TRO letting the city start the Truth in Taxation steps. It did not approve any tax.
  • The ruling on the merits: July 13, Judge Todd Shaughnessy found the rule enforceable: "The Rule is enforceable, the city was created one day too late through no fault of its own" (as reported by Ogden Valley News).
  • Appeal: On Aug. 5, the Utah Supreme Court denied the city's request for immediate relief as moot, and did not rule on whether the city's argument was right (Ogden Valley News). That report is current through Aug. 12. The Hive has not checked the Supreme Court docket since.

What each side says

QuestionOgden Valley CityUtah State Tax Commission
The core argumentState law should override the Commission's administrative rule (KSL, June 15).The Jan. 1 date "is the linchpin of the timing of Utah's property tax system" (KSL, July 6).
Whose fault was the delay?It "urgently advised that later issuance would jeopardize its ability to secure needed property taxes in 2026" (city's lawsuit, as quoted by KSL).Not reported.
Why not make an exception?The delay was outside the city's control.An exception "opens the door to other entities making the argument and upending the property tax assessment" (KSL, July 6).

What court rulings and records confirm

  • Judge Shaughnessy's July 13 ruling, as reported: the rule is enforceable, state law is "silent on the precise question raised here," and the delay "was due to circumstances that were essentially beyond the City's control" (KUER).
  • The June 22 TRO did not decide the case or approve any tax (Ogden Valley News; FOX 13).
  • The Supreme Court's Aug. 5 order denied immediate relief as moot and "did not rule that the City's legal argument was wrong" (Ogden Valley News, Aug. 16).
  • The conditional budget would raise the city rate from 0.000156 to 0.000953; on a $1.22 million home, from $104.85 to $640.51 (Ogden Valley News, Aug. 28).

Claims no court has decided

These are what a party says in its filings. They are not findings.

  • The city alleges state law overrides the Commission's Jan. 1 rule. The Supreme Court has not decided this.

What it means for the city's budget

If the city loses, it stays on its interim budget, funded by the 6% municipal energy tax and a planned Transportation Utility Fee. A councilmember estimated the fee at $20 to $60 per home per month (Ogden Valley News, Aug. 28). The mayor said, "It's not a flush budget" (KSL, Aug. 19).

Property tax is collected once a year, in the fall. Ogden Valley News reported the Supreme Court denied immediate relief because county notices had already gone out without the city's rate.

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

  1. Utah State Tax Commission: Has the Jan. 1 rule ever been waived for another new entity?
  2. Lieutenant Governor's Office: Why did the certificate take from Dec. 17 to Jan. 2, and is there a deadline for processing incorporation papers?
  3. Utah Legislature: Should state law set a clear start date for new cities' taxing power?
  4. Ogden Valley City Council: What will the FY27 budget look like if the Supreme Court rules against the city?
