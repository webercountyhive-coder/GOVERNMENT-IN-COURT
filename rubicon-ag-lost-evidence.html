CASE FILE · GOVERNMENT IN COURT · UTAH ATTORNEY GENERAL'S OFFICE · GC 001

Seized, held for two and a half years, then missing: the Rubicon evidence lawsuit

A Bountiful company and its owners say the Utah Attorney General's Office lost a box of original papers and four USB drives it seized in a 2023 raid. The state's case against them was dismissed, and federal prosecutors declined to bring charges. The Attorney General's Office says the items went missing while federal agents had legal custody, and it has asked the court to dismiss the suit.

Court: Third District, Salt Lake CountyCase No. 260907235Filed Sept. 15, 2026Status: motion to dismiss reported filed Nov. 4

Posted Nov. 6, 2026

WHERE · WHO
Utah Attorney General's Office · Third District Court (Salt Lake) · Second District Court (Davis) · U.S. District Court (Utah)
  • Who is suing: Rudy and Jena Larsen and three of their companies: Rubicon Contracting, Scandia Company and Smart Rain Systems (Bountiful).
  • Who is being sued: The Office of the Utah Attorney General, as an agency. No person is named as a defendant in this case.
  • What it is about: A box of original documents and four USB drives seized Nov. 20, 2023, that could not be found on May 21, 2026.
  • The judge: Heather Brereton, Third District Court.
  • What came before: A state human trafficking case (dismissed without prejudice, February 2025), a federal investigation (closed with no charges, April 2026), and a $1 billion federal civil rights suit (pending).

In plain words (explain it like I'm 10)

In 2023, state investigators searched a company's offices and took boxes of papers and computer drives. They said the owners were mistreating foreign workers. The state case never went to trial. A judge dropped it in 2025 when the state asked, and federal prosecutors later decided not to charge anyone. When it came time to hand the seized things over, one box of papers and four USB drives could not be found. Now the owners are suing the Attorney General's Office. They want the things back, or an explanation and money. The Attorney General's Office says federal agents were in charge of the evidence when it went missing, and it wants the judge to throw the case out. Nobody has been found guilty of anything. A judge has not decided this lawsuit.

The short version

  • The charges: In November 2023 the AG's Office charged Rubicon's founder and six executives with 14 counts each of aggravated human trafficking (KSL). The state asked to dismiss in February 2025, saying it "defers to the Department of Justice and its pending investigation." The judge dismissed without prejudice, which means the charges could be refiled.
  • No federal charges: A Department of Justice letter dated April 8, 2026, says "We declined prosecution, as has the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Utah." The letter became public through Rubicon's court filing.
  • The missing items: One box of original papers and four USB drives. The owners say they were last logged in the AG's evidence storage and could not be produced at a federal evidence pickup on May 21, 2026.
  • The two sides disagree on who had the evidence. The complaint quotes a May 4, 2026 sworn declaration by an assistant attorney general saying the evidence "physically remained in possession of the UAGO." The AG's Office's Nov. 4, 2026 motion says Homeland Security Investigations held it from December 2024 until May 21, 2026.
  • Not decided: No court has ruled on this lawsuit, on the $1 billion federal suit, or on whether any statement in the original search warrants was false.

What a Franks hearing is

Before police can search a place, an officer swears to a judge, in writing, why there is good reason to search. A Franks hearing (named for the 1978 U.S. Supreme Court case Franks v. Delaware) is when the person who was searched asks the judge to check whether that sworn statement told the truth. If the officer knowingly lied or left out key facts, and the warrant would not have been approved without them, the search can be thrown out.

A judge only grants the hearing after a "substantial showing" that something may be wrong. Granting the hearing is not a ruling that the statements were false.

What Judge Cornish wrote (as reported by KSL, July 19, 2024)The defense made a "substantial showing" that the agent's statements were "knowingly false or at a minimum made with a reckless disregard for the truth," and the affidavits may be "replete with false and misleading statements."
What the defendants' Feb. 11, 2026 federal motion saysThe complaint does not allege that the evidentiary hearing occurred, so the judge "did not determine whether any of the statements in the affidavits actually were false."

The complaint quotes the ruling as saying it is "possible, perhaps even probable" that the affidavits are "replete with false and misleading statements." In one place (¶ 55) the complaint drops the "possible, perhaps even probable" part. This page keeps it.

Both can be partly true: the complaint itself says HSI had legal custody after the December 2024 federal warrant, while the items sat in an AG's Office locker. What the records don't show yet is when the items were last seen, who opened that locker, and whether the federal agent's sworn return listed every item.   • The AG's Office accepted service Sept. 21, 2026 (filing).
  • Seven Rubicon leaders were charged in November 2023 with 14 counts each of aggravated human trafficking (KSL).
  • A Franks hearing was granted on 5 of 10 warrants in July 2024 (KSL).

  • A federal Office for Victims of Crime award, 15POVC-24-GK-01059-HT, for the Utah Trafficking in Persons Task Force: $999,994, posted Sept. 20, 2024 (OVC). The award page does not name the receiving agency.

What is only alleged (not decided by any court)

These come from the complaint and the Notice of Claim. They are the Larsens' claims.

  • The complaint alleges the AG's Office "manufactured a human-trafficking prosecution to obtain additional funding."
  • The complaint alleges the case agent kept privileged records in his office, checked out all evidence after a court order to stop review, and left "a hole in the chain of custody."
  • The Notice of Claim alleges a forensic exam the Larsens paid for shows a seized laptop was powered on and more than 1,000 files were written to it while in AG custody.
  • The complaint alleges senior AG staff encouraged federal agencies to charge the Larsens after the state case ended.
  • The complaint alleges the AG's victim count rose from 26 in 2022 to 310 in 2023, "driven substantially" by this case, and was used to seek funding.
  • The complaint alleges the AG's Office filed Mr. Larsen's personal identifiers unredacted in another case and later sent him a "Data Breach" letter (March 4, 2026). The Hive does not publish those details.

The program and its funding

The case came out of the AG's SECURE Strike Force, a unit in its Criminal Investigations Division. The Legislature created it in 2009 (complaint, ¶ 72, citing H.B. 64, 2009).

The complaint says federal grant awards in September 2024 sent $999,994 to the AG's Office and $949,999 to the Asian Association of Utah. The Hive confirmed the first award on the federal OVC site: 15POVC-24-GK-01059-HT, $999,994, for the Utah Trafficking in Persons Task Force. The second award has not been checked yet.

Whether this case was used to win funding is the Larsens' claim. The award pages do not mention it.

What we don't know yet

  • When the box and the four drives were last seen, and by whom.
  • Whether the AG's Office's evidence log ("Versaterm Evidence Continuity," per the complaint) shows a checkout with no return.
  • Whether any copy or image of the drives' data exists.
  • Whether the Franks hearing was ever held. The defense's federal motion says the complaint doesn't allege it was.
  • How the judge ruled at the preliminary hearing, if she did, before the case was dismissed.
  • Whether the second federal grant ($949,999 to the Asian Association of Utah) matches the complaint.
  • How the judges will rule on the motions to dismiss in this case and the federal case.

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

  1. The Utah Legislature's Judiciary and Law Enforcement committees: What rules require state agencies to log, store and return seized property, and who checks that they follow them?
  2. The Legislative Auditor General: Has any audit looked at how the AG's Office tracks seized evidence since the January 2025 audits?
  3. The Attorney General's Office (public records): What written policy does the office have for storing seized property, as Utah Code § 77-11a-204 requires?
  4. Third District Court (court docket): When is the hearing on the AG's motion to dismiss?
  5. Readers: When an agency seizes property and a case ends without charges, how long should it take to get the property back?
